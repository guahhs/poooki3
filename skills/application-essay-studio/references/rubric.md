# Rubric

Thirteen dimensions. Score the first eleven 1–5; the last two are pass/fail
gates reported in Flags. Scale anchors are drafting practice, not a validated
instrument — there is no inter-rater-reliability study of admissions essay
ratings, so treat scores as a way to force specificity, not as measurement.

**Evidence before score, every time.** Quote the span, name the finding, then
rate. Rating first produces scores that cluster at the top; this ordering is one
of the few judge-calibration fixes with primary support behind it.

**Scoring is optional, and often not worth it.** Testing against an expert
reviewer's own markup found the numbers added nothing: that reviewer made zero
scoring judgements across eighty-five comments and out-performed the scored run,
while the scale generated some of its nitpicks. Treat these thirteen rows
primarily as a **checklist of what to look for**, and attach numbers only when
the applicant wants to compare drafts across rounds or the committee needs a
defensible ranking. A named finding with a quotation always beats a score.

**Scale**, when used. 1 actively damaging · 2 a real defect · 3 competent and
forgettable · 4 working · 5 the reason this file gets advocated for. Most drafts
sit at 2–3. A 5 should be rare; if more than two dimensions score 5, re-read as
a sceptic before reporting.

---

## 1. Theme and focus

One sharp central claim about the writer. Everything serves it.

Failure: reads as a résumé in paragraph form; three stories crammed into one;
a theme stated in the final line that the body never earned.

Diagnostic: if the essay cannot be titled, the theme is not yet sharp. Ask the
applicant to title it — inability to is the finding.

## 2. Protagonism

The applicant is the actor, not the witness.

Failure: inspired-by-someone essays where the other person performs all the
action; heritage or family mentioned once and then abandoned.

Mechanically checkable: count sentences whose grammatical subject is someone
other than the applicant. A high proportion in the middle section is the signal.

## 3. Show versus tell

Specific anecdote, concrete detail, active verb.

Failure, and name the variant: vague generalisation; achievement listing; and
**self-praise** — naming your own virtue instead of demonstrating it. "This was
my first proof of resilience" tells the reader which quality to credit. Cut the
label; the incident either carries it or does not.

Diagnostic: per paragraph, what does this say about the applicant? If nothing,
it probably does not belong.

## 4. Reflection

Space spent on why it mattered, and reflection *earned* rather than asserted.

Failure: recounting events with no shift; an insight announced but not
demonstrated; the motivational-poster ending.

Do **not** flag "I realized" or "that's when I understood" as defects in
themselves. Published guidance is split, and the source requiring visible
reflection serves low-income applicants. The defect is an unearned realisation,
not the phrase introducing it.

## 5. Authenticity and voice

Sounds like this person and no one else.

Failure: thesaurus reach; register far above the applicant's own writing;
sentence rhythm that never varies.

When voice and polish conflict, **voice wins**. Over-formality is a defect even
when it reads as better writing. Compare against the applicant's own unedited
sample where one exists; without a sample, say that the voice judgement is
unanchored rather than guessing.

## 6. Authority of stance

A statement, not a plea. The writer asserts rather than seeking permission.

Failure: hedging stacked around every claim; apologising for the material;
justifying the right to an opinion. Distinct from authenticity — prose can be
honest and still deferential.

Checkable: hedge density. *seems, tends, may, might, suggests, somewhat, I
think, perhaps, kind of.* Flag clusters, not individual instances.

## 7. Distinctiveness and non-substitutability

Could another applicant have written this sentence?

Two tests, both mechanical:

- **Sentence substitution.** Take any claim. Could a thousand other applicants
  write it verbatim? "I dedicated all of myself to studying" passes nothing —
  plenty of people work hard.
- **Institution substitution** (代替可能性). Swap the institution's name for a
  competitor's. If the text still stands, it has demonstrated nothing about
  this programme.

Cliché decomposes into three independently checkable layers: **common topic,
common connections, common language.** A common topic is permitted and only
raises the execution bar. Common *connections* — the expected value extracted
from the expected experience — are the real defect.

**When this scores low, the output is a question, not a deduction.** Name the
sentence and ask what would make it only theirs. Genericness correlates with
having had less editing help, so a bare penalty here falls hardest on the
applicants who need the most.

## 8. Causal chain

Motive, preparation and future plan present *and causally linked*.

Failure (断絶): all three elements appear, none connects to the others. Past
study does not explain the choice of this faculty; the future plan does not
follow from either.

This is the backbone dimension for 志望理由書-descended prompts, where the
chain is what the prompt explicitly asks for. See
`references/japan-english-medium.md`.

## 9. Opening

First sentence carries the most specific concrete fact available.

Failure: windup and abstract scene-setting; a quotation; a dictionary or
encyclopaedia definition; a general statement about the human condition;
background exposition before anything happens.

**Do not prescribe an unexplained literary tableau.** Leading with a specific
fact and withholding the point are different moves. Where the prompt or budget
demands brevity, deferring the claim is a defect — see the Japanese reference
file, where an explicit brevity directive makes the atmospheric cold open
actively costly.

## 10. Landing

The final slot does the work the genre reserves for it.

For 志望理由書-descended and study-plan prompts, that slot belongs to the
forward-looking element. A circular return to the opening image competes for it
and usually loses — flag the displacement, not the device.

For open personal-statement prompts, a close that returns to the opening image
with new understanding is a genuine strength.

## 11. Tone and red flags

Failure: bitterness toward named people; score-settling; contempt; petty
grievance; self-pity presented as hardship. A reader who nearly cut you from a
team becoming the essay's antagonist is a character risk independent of craft.

Also here: hardship written from the wound rather than the scar. Material about
difficulty should run heavily on what changed, not on the damage. Where an
applicant is a minor and the material involves abuse or self-harm, note that
disclosure can trigger mandatory reporting obligations on the receiving side —
raise it as a consideration, never as an instruction to suppress.

## 12. Prompt coverage — gate

Split the prompt into every clause and check each independently. A prompt naming
two fields requires both. A prompt asking what you did *and* what you learned
requires both. Report clause by clause, met or not. Any uncovered clause is a
Flag, not a rubric row.

## 13. Mechanics — gate

Grammar, spelling, consistency of tense and name. A floor, not a virtue. Several
admissions bodies state plainly that grammar is not evaluated. Suppress mechanics
findings unless they block comprehension or signal haste — a misspelled programme
name belongs in Flags.

---

## Reporting

Score only dimensions the draft gives you evidence for; mark the rest
`not assessable` and say what would make them assessable. An honest gap beats a
fabricated 3.

Lead the rubric block with the two or three lowest scores. The budget of seven
findings per essay per round comes out of those, not spread evenly across
thirteen rows.
