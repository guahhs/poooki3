# Accuracy pass

Most factual failures in application essays are not false statements. They are
true statements doing work they cannot support. Checking only truth misses the
majority of them.

Check four layers for every claim specific enough to be right or wrong:
statistics, rankings, named studies, named institutions or people, historical
and scientific facts, translated terms, institutional achievements.

## Layer 1 — Truth

Is the stated fact correct? Search for the original source rather than trusting
the number as written. Flag bare assertions with no source behind them.

## Layer 2 — Metric match

**Does the measure cited support the claim being made?** This is the most common
defect and the easiest to miss, because the claim looks true and a headline will
confirm it.

Worked example of the shape, not of the answer. A draft claims a city
"frequently ranks among the world's most polluted." Two different measures exist
for that: real-time air-quality snapshots, which are volatile and can put a city
first on a bad winter day, and annual-average rankings, which are stable and
usually place it very differently. Both are real, they measure different things,
and the sentence silently borrows the dramatic measure's authority for a general
claim. A naive check reads a real-time headline and marks the claim verified.

**Fetch both numbers; do not supply either from memory.** Rankings move every
year, and a remembered figure here would violate invariant 9 as surely as any
other unverified fact. Then write the fix so it says which measure is which and
over what period — an annual-average position with its concentration figure and
the WHO guideline multiple, plus a separate clause for the real-time behaviour
if the applicant wants to keep it. Same subject, defensible sentence.

The general rule: when two measures exist for one phenomenon, establish which
one the claim needs, fetch that one, and make the sentence name the period or
basis it is talking about.

When two different measures exist for one phenomenon, say which one the claim
needs and whether it holds under that one.

## Layer 3 — Scope

Does the evidence support the breadth asserted? A single example does not
support "uniquely", "the best", "the most important", or "the only". Where a
claim is used as evidence for a broader argument, check whether the example
actually reaches the scope of the argument — every individual sentence can be
true while the inference between them fails.

Superlatives in a thesis need a defence, not a citation. If the draft claims one
trend is *the most important*, the applicant must be able to say why the obvious
rival is less important.

## Layer 4 — Mechanism

Is the underlying process described correctly? A domain specialist will flag a
wrong mechanism immediately — and may well be the interviewer.

Worked example. A draft claims that converting CO2 into fuels "reduces
atmospheric carbon levels". The chemistry does not support it: fuel made from CO2
re-releases that CO2 on combustion, so fuels recycle carbon rather than removing
it. Only long-lived products — polymers, concrete and minerals — store it. The
corrected phrasing is that it *cuts new fossil carbon*, which is both true and
more precise. A reviewer in the field would question that sentence without
hesitation.

Mechanism errors are the most damaging class, because they undermine the
applicant's claim to understand their own subject. Check them first in any essay
that argues a technical position.

## Institutional credit claims

A special case worth its own check. Claims that attach an institution to a prize,
discovery or ranking need the affiliation verified **at the time of the
achievement**, not merely an association at some point. A laureate who studied or
later worked somewhere was not necessarily there when the work was done or the
award given, and a faculty reader will know.

The same applies to claimed courses, labs, professors and research groups: verify
against the programme's own published materials. See the programme research step
in SKILL.md.

## Interview defensibility

After the four layers, list every technical term and claim the applicant would
have to explain unaided in an interview. Ask them to explain each one without
notes. Anything they cannot own in conversation is a liability regardless of
whether it is true — and at least one university states explicitly that
applicants may be asked about their essay answers.

This is also the most honest test available of whether the expertise in a draft
belongs to the applicant.

## Reporting

Every correction ships with:

- the quoted span,
- which layer failed and precisely what is wrong,
- replacement wording that is accurate and fits the word limit,
- a source URL.

Where a claim cannot be verified after a reasonable search, report it as
**unverified** and say what would settle it. Where web access is unavailable,
report the whole accuracy pass as unverified and say so prominently. Never score
accuracy from memory — a silently unverified fact is worse than an acknowledged
gap, because the applicant stops checking.

## Research leads

The accuracy pass should leave the applicant more expert, not just more correct.
Where a draft gestures at a subject the applicant clearly cares about, supply
source URLs that would let them understand it properly — primary literature,
government and agency programme pages, review articles. An applicant who has read
three real sources on their own topic writes differently, and interviews better,
than one who has been handed corrected sentences.
