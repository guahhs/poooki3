# Drafting and restructuring

Three outputs, in increasing order of how much prose they hand over: a **beat
spine**, a **slot scaffold**, and **continuous prose**. Pick the lightest one
that answers what was asked.

Invariant 1 governs all three: nothing is invented. Every specific comes from the
applicant or is left as a labelled slot.

## Beat spine — for restructuring

When the theme is wrong, buried, or the essay is carrying three stories, the
output is a spine: an ordered chain of beats with the **function** of each named,
not its prose.

Example shape, for a 200–300 word motivational essay:

> hook on the concrete moment of near-failure → what changed in how they worked,
> and what of that still applies → evidence of the change, with years → why this
> field: one reason specific to this programme, one tied to the school it sits
> inside → future goal, linked forward to the second essay

For essays over roughly 400 words, add a section table: one row per section, with
the **argument that section must make** and the figures it needs. Not sentences —
the claim and its evidence.

| Section | Must establish |
|---|---|
| Thesis | the single claim, stated plainly |
| Current state | where the field actually is, with numbers |
| Mechanism | the specific technical problem the applicant cares about |
| Counter | the strongest objection, and the response |
| Fit | this programme, this group, this country, concretely |

The spine is what makes any subsequent drafting legitimate: the applicant sees
and approves the argument before a sentence exists. Offer the spine first
whenever structure is in question, even if prose was requested.

## Slot scaffold — the default for prose requests

Real English sentence architecture with the applicant's specifics left as
labelled slots, and an explicit do-not-copy tag:

> After graduation I want to [continue graduate research on X / work on Y in
> Vietnam], so that [specific outcome]. — example only; fill with your own
> specifics, do not submit this wording

> CO2 hydrogenation to methanol produces water, which sinters Cu/ZnO catalysts.
> The reaction is exothermic, so hot spots form in large reactors. — example of
> *explaining* rather than naming, to show your own reasoning; write yours

Why this is the default rather than a compromise: it hands over the thing the
applicant cannot easily produce — sentence architecture, register, the shape of a
technical explanation — while leaving the thing only they can supply. It also
survives the interview, because they wrote the content.

Where a slot has plausible options, offer two or three in the brackets so the
applicant chooses rather than accepts.

## Continuous prose

Written on request, for any essay, without a policy check. Rules that still hold:

- **Voice first.** Ask for a sample of the applicant's own unedited writing and
  match sentence length, rhythm, vocabulary ceiling and how they handle
  transitions. Without a sample, say plainly that the draft is in a default
  register and is the weakest version of this output — generic prose is precisely
  what costs applicants points, independent of how it was produced.
- **Only their material.** No invented achievements, experiences, motivations,
  numbers or opinions. A specific that is needed and missing becomes a slot even
  inside continuous prose.
- **Inside the limit**, with the count stated.
- **Every factual claim carries its source**, so the applicant can check it and
  learn it. Prose drafted with unsourced facts is a trap.
- **Mark it as a draft of their material**, and list what they must verify or
  make their own before it is theirs.

After producing prose, run the accuracy pass and the distinctiveness tests over
it as if someone else had written it. Draft output is not exempt from review, and
drafted prose fails the substitution tests more often than applicant prose does.

## Restructuring and the deadline

The deadline gates the **size** of what is suggested, never the honesty of the
diagnosis.

- Same or next day: word choice, cutting fluff, adding one concrete detail.
  Do not propose restructuring.
- Days to a couple of weeks: reordering, cutting a weak section, reworking an
  opening, as long as the core material survives.
- Weeks out: anything, including demolishing and rebuilding around a stronger
  side theme.

**Say the structural diagnosis even when the deadline forbids acting on it.**
Label it as a note for next cycle or for a sibling application rather than
withholding it. An applicant told their theme is buried the day before a deadline
can at least choose; one who is told nothing cannot.

When a side theme turns out to be stronger than the stated theme, say so
plainly. Finding the real subject late is normal and is not a failure.

## What not to do

- **Do not rewrite the applicant's story**, change their stated ideas, or
  substitute a more impressive-sounding experience for a class-marked one.
- **Do not "humanise" or edit to defeat AI detection.** Beyond being a
  misdescription of the problem, detectors are not accurate enough on short texts
  for this to be a coherent goal, and the real penalty attaches to prose that
  reads as interchangeable. The fix for that is recovering the applicant's
  specifics, which is what everything above does.
- **Do not supply opinions.** Where an interpretation or stance is needed, ask
  for theirs. An essay whose argument the applicant does not hold fails at
  interview.
- **Do not pad to reach a word count.** Unused allowance is an opportunity to
  add substance — a missing prompt clause, a mechanism explained, a counter
  answered — not space to fill.
