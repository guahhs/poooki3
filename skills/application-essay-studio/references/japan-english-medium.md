# Japanese institutions, English-medium applications

For international applicants writing in English to Japanese universities, and for
study and research plans. Read before judging structure for any Japanese
institution.

Fetch current prompts and limits at the time of use. The examples below are
evidence of **genre shape**, not data to reuse.

## The genre is hybrid, and that is the whole point

A US personal statement optimises narrative. A Japanese 志望理由書 optimises a
demonstrated chain. English-medium prompts at Japanese universities ask for the
chain, in English, and will not reward narrative that arrives without it.

The prompts say so themselves. Nagoya's compulsory first essay, 200–300 words,
asks the applicant to explain *why* they are applying to a particular programme
and *how it relates* to their academic history and their personal and
professional future goals — three elements, with the relation between them
explicitly required — prefaced by "as briefly and clearly as possible". Waseda
SILS asks applicants to argue a position on supplied academic passages, then
explain the choice from their own academic experience and link it to future study
at SILS **citing specific courses**, with syllabus URLs provided. Nagoya's
Biological Science prompt requires naming **one research group** read off the
department website.

So: **the unit of academic fit is a named research group or course, not a
discipline.**

## Two documents, two voices

Most of these applications contain a motivational essay and a second essay that
is an academic reasoning task — interpret a graph and say why your own
explanation is insufficient; argue a position and identify the stakeholders;
analyse one example and name the tension that remains.

Judge them differently. The motivational essay carries the chain and the person.
The reasoning essay is assessed as thinking: claim, evidence, mechanism, and the
honesty to name what is unresolved. Narrative craft findings mostly do not apply
to the second, and a reasoning essay that opens with a personal anecdote is
usually spending its budget wrongly.

## Three US heuristics that do not transfer

**The unexplained cold open hurts.** Evidence: explicit brevity directives inside
200–300 word budgets; the EJU writing rubric rewards a claim being 明確に述べ
られている and zero-scores off-task work; a Kyushu Institute of Technology rubric
scores paragraph-topic structure answering the question; Japanese guidance names
感動アピール型 — the emotional-appeal essay — as a known failure mode.

This does not mean a flat opening is fine. Lead with the most specific concrete
fact available. The distinction is between front-loading a specific and
withholding the point: "in grade 9 my chemistry teacher discussed replacing me on
the olympiad team" states a fact immediately and survives the brevity test; an
atmospheric tableau that defers the claim does not.

**The circular close hurts by displacement.** The final slot in this genre is
reserved for the forward-looking element — 卒業後の展望, the contribution the
work would make — confirmed across several sources including Osaka University's
own guidance. A return to the opening image competes for that slot and loses.
Flag the displacement rather than the device.

**"Don't list coursework" is backwards here.** Naming specific courses,
professors and research groups is the *mechanism* for demonstrating
non-substitutability. Verify each name against the programme's published
materials; an invented or misnamed course is unforced and fatal. Applicants also
routinely give the field name instead of the programme name, which is itself a
finding.

**Humour: no verdict.** No evidence was found either way, in English or Japanese.
Do not flag it as a defect and do not recommend it.

## Two named failure modes

**代替可能性 — substitutability.** Swap the institution's name for a
competitor's. If the text still stands, it has failed. Mechanically checkable and
the sharpest single test in this genre.

**断絶 — disconnection.** Motive, learning and future plan all present, none
causally linked to the others. The most common structural failure, and invisible
unless you trace the links explicitly.

## Study and research plans

Five elements, with 研究目的 — the purpose — described in Osaka University's
guidance as 最も重視される部分, the part weighted most heavily.

The background move has three parts, in order: what prior work settled, what the
applicant has settled, and **what remains unclarified**. The third part is where
most drafts stop short, and it is the part that demonstrates the applicant can
locate their own work.

**Feasibility within the stated period is an explicit criterion, so
over-scoping is a defect.** This inverts US research-statement craft, which
rewards ambition. A plan that could not be completed in the time available
counts against the applicant.

The same logic governs the MEXT field-of-study-and-research-plan document, and a
200-character compression of this skeleton works well as the spine for a 200–300
word motivational essay.

## Integrity rules, as context for the applicant

Report these as facts when a programme is named; they are the applicant's
operating environment.

Rules are set **per programme**, not per university, and are heterogeneous.
Nagoya prohibits generative AI for essays and states that essays are run through
AI-detection software, with most programmes carrying a ban while at least one
permits AI for proofreading only. Waseda SILS states AI use may be considered
cheating. Yamanashi treats verbatim use as 不正行為 with admission revoked.
Nagoya's guidance also notes applicants may be asked about their answers at
interview, and cram-school teachers are explicitly not accepted as referees.

The finding that matters most for craft rather than compliance: research
presented at an NII symposium in 2025 found documents evaluators merely
*perceived* as AI-written scored lower **regardless of whether AI was used**,
that detectors are not accurate enough for admissions decisions on short texts,
and that using AI as a discussion partner compressed the score distribution until
the document stopped discriminating between candidates. Independent work on a
graduate admissions cohort found the penalty for AI-written essays *rose* once
essay quality was controlled for — readers are penalising genericness, not bad
writing.

The practical consequence for this skill: prose that reads as interchangeable
costs the applicant points whatever its origin. That is a quality argument for
recovering their specifics, and it is why voice matching matters.

## Verified gaps

Do not fill these from memory. Fetch, or say you could not.

- UTokyo PEAK requirements could not be retrieved; a circulating word-count
  figure traces to a 2017 blog and should not be used.
- Kyoto iUP essay prompt not extracted, though the form is confirmed to exist.
- Keio PEARL word limits were not verifiable from Keio's own pages.
- MEXT's own form could not be reached; the commonly cited page limit rests on a
  single commercial guide.
- **No official source states that undershooting a word limit is penalised.** The
  convention of filling at least 90% is cram-school advice. Report an unused
  allowance as opportunity cost, not as a violation.
- No published "why Japan" or "commitment to staying" criterion was found for any
  English-medium undergraduate programme checked, despite agency material
  asserting one exists. Do not invent the criterion to score against.
- No evidence of differential assessment by nationality was found. Vietnam is the
  third-largest origin country for international students in Japan.

**Read document headers, not filenames.** At least one university serves its
current-year essay questions at a URL naming the previous year while the document
header states the correct one.
